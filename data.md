# Montreal Walking Tour: Syrian and Lebanese Heritage Tourism

## Location 1: St. George Antiochian Orthodox Church

- **Description**: It is a Byzantine church that has towers that are domed and have multicolored murals. It is the heart of the Syrian-Lebanese society, an embodiment of continuity by transition.
- **Analysis**: The church has a combination of Western and Byzantine architecture and it can observe cultural negotiation. Its murals symbolize the re-possessing of the identity in the new land. It is an external expression of the community strength of the sacred place where the traditions are still living in Montreal. This church serves as the map of brand to the diaspora physical landmarks that enable communities to find their way through belonging despite displacement to mark temporal strata of arrival and settlement.
- **Field Notes**: The twin domes having cross at the top are the characteristic features in Villeroy. It is a Byzantine and Romanesque structure constructed in 1939-40 by an architect called Raoul Gariepy, as a symbol of the two-fold heritage of the community. The inside murals by Emmanuel Briffa are a depiction of the Orthodox traditions and the liturgy that was used during the construction remains the same.

## Location 2: Jean-Talon Market Area

- **Description**: A crowded market full of food stalls and shops offering spices and products of Middle Eastern origin. The historic site also includes the Sayfy Grocery which is a pre-historic economic hub of the Syrian-Lebanese immigrants. Les Filles Fattoush, a social enterprise created by Syrian refugee women, has a permanent kiosk nowadays, which is an extension of the tradition of community entrepreneurship and maintenance of culinary traditions.
- **Analysis**: The market was established in 1903 and it is the primary public market centre in Montreal. The sellers are selling zaatar, hummus, fresh herbs and spices, which are sensory signifiers of home. In line with the idea of walking the city brought forward by De Certeau, the way people of this community are negotiating this market is not as consumers, but as agents who regain the urban space by means of multilingual trade and sensory intimacy.
- **Field Notes**: The observation of vendors showing known foods in structured stalls. French, Arabic and English multilingual signs. Les Filles Fattoush kiosk depicts women in the economic involvement and food transfer. Social bonds and knowledge sharing is manifested in informal interactions between vendors and customers.

## Location 3: Le Petit Alep Restaurant

- **Description**: A Middle Eastern exotic restaurant at the side of the market. It glorifies the Syrian and Armenian family principles in food and has been an iconic restaurant since 1995.
- **Analysis**: The restaurant is a living museum, and it stores the narratives and tastes of the community. Food is made a cultural preservation and memory of the people since it gives them a shared experience. The route linking the market to the restaurant is a practice of tactics in which immigrants create significant routes within the city that alters commercial avenues into lived manifestations of cultural continuity.
- **Field Notes**: The interior decor is Middle-Eastern with cozy lights and classic furniture. Family recipes that have been passed through generations are shown in the menu.  Restaurant serves as the meeting place of various clients like artists, students, and members of the community.

## Location 4: St. Nicholas Orthodox Church of Antiochia

- **Description**: It was founded in 1899, the original Antiochian Orthodox parish in Canada. It is the oldest institutional continuity of the Syrian Orthodox Christianity in North America 40 years preceding St. George.
- **Analysis**: The identity formation of early diaspora of St. Nicholas is founded on the institution of building of religion. Being a map of the diaspora, this church enabled the early communities to find their way of belonging even when they were displaced, and this formed the basis of the subsequent institutional growth.
- **Field Notes**: The church was built in 1899 at the de Castelnau Street by Fr. Raphael who in 1920s founded the Syrian Ladies Benevolent Society.The origins of immigrant population in the working classes can be seen in its plain building of bricks, which is not as elegant as the building of St. George.

## Location 5: Salahouddine Mosque

- **Description**: It was built in 1990 as a volunteer-built mosque of a former marble factory, and it represents the Muslim Syrian-Lebanese diaspora of the late 20th century, specifically the one who emigrated in 1986.
- **Analysis**: The mosque portrays the diversity of the diaspora in terms of time and religion. Salahouddine is the symbol of the late 20th-century Muslim immigration, including the Lebanese Shia and the Syrian refugees. It is a reflection of the 1994 model of diaspora as heterogeneous worked out by Hall, the same Syrian-Lebanese origin, the other religious origin, the other period of history. Being a late comer institution, the mosque reflects the idea of diaspora proposed in the analysis by Brand as something that constantly remakes itself, introducing new doorways and new maps into the spatial vocabulary of the community of home.
- **Field Notes**: Former marble workers converted the factory into a prayer space in 1990, having given up their labor as they donated their efforts in building the factory. The mosque is symbolic of contemporary day diaspora refugee settlement, which turns the economic precarity into spiritual and social power.
